\paragraph{Rebalancing depends on the dataset and on the deployment.}
The ablation above measures rebalancing on test sets drawn from the training distribution.
The second recipe, which applies no rebalancing, adds two comparisons: square-root oversampling as an alternative to EMLA, and the cost of rebalancing under shift.
On Pl@ntNet300K both methods pay (\cref{tab:longtail}).
Square-root oversampling raises every head, by 1.3 to 9.7 points, and removes the conditional head's deficit; EMLA gives the best flat head (+4.8) but lowers the conditional head by 6.2 points, as in the main comparison, where the conditional head trails on Pl@ntNet300K.

On Global Lepidoptera EMLA buys 0.6 points on the test fold (hierarchical head, 0.894 against a three-seed mean of 0.887) and costs 3.1 points on the trap images (0.300 against 0.331), below all three seeds of the unweighted model.
What a model learns from a species' few dozen photographs is largely about those photographs (one photographer, background and camera); up-weighting them helps on a test set that shares those artefacts and can hurt on images that do not.
The trap scores of the main comparison, all trained with EMLA, may therefore be lower than an unweighted recipe would give.

The two training sets differ in how heavy their tail is.
In Pl@ntNet300K the median species has 22 training images, 62\,\% of species have fewer than 50, and the effective number of classes, $\exp(H)$ for the entropy $H$ of the class frequencies, is 19\,\% of the number of classes.
In Global Lepidoptera, whose corpus is capped at about 2,000 images per species, the median species has 163, 10\,\% have fewer than 50, and the effective number of classes is 54\,\%.
Rebalancing is worth most for a training set with a heavy tail, for instance when most classes have fewer than 50 images or the effective number of classes is below a third of the total, and is worth testing on target images before a model is deployed under a domain shift.
Two datasets cannot place these thresholds precisely; they mark where each of our datasets falls.
